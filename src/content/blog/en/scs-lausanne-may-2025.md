At the SCS Congress in Lausanne, held at EPFL and convening Switzerland's surgical community, Dr. Alice Gilson from the Department of Visceral Surgery at HUG in Geneva addressed the growing administrative burden in medicine and the role artificial intelligence could play in transforming medical documentation. The talk did not stay at the level of futurism. It started from a number Swiss clinicians recognize too well: physicians can spend up to five hours per day on administrative tasks. Against that backdrop, interest in an [AI medical scribe](/ai-medical-scribe/) is less about novelty and more about reclaiming a workable clinical day.

![DocNote at SCS Congress in Lausanne](/images/SCS%20Lausanne.jpeg)

Citing recent Swiss data, Dr. Gilson identified key drivers of the load: poorly designed EHR systems, rising regulatory requirements, medico-legal traceability, and complex clinical coding obligations. Early studies suggest AI-powered documentation tools can reduce note-writing time by around 20% and improve quality, while still requiring careful review. She also stressed associated risks, including hallucinations, clinical responsibility, and data protection under Swiss and EU law. The session closed by presenting DocNote, a Swiss-developed approach designed to generate anonymised consultation reports with strict local data storage and automatic deletion of recordings. What follows expands that Lausanne framing into adoption lessons surgical and hospital teams can use.

## The Swiss admin burden is a systems problem, not a willpower problem

When congress audiences hear "five hours," the reaction is rarely surprise. It is recognition. Surgical clinics, wards, and on-call patterns already compress teaching and recovery time. Documentation expands into evenings because the day cannot absorb it. Treating that as an individual productivity failure misses the structural causes Dr. Gilson listed:

- **EHR friction**: too many clicks, duplicate fields, and templates that fight natural narrative flow
- **Regulatory expansion**: more mandatory fields, audits, and reporting without more clinical time
- **Medico-legal traceability**: charts that must reconstruct why a decision was made months later
- **Coding complexity**: billing and classification rules that turn clinical language into another dialect

AI documentation does not repeal those forces. It can absorb some of the typing and structuring work so physicians spend attention on decisions and patients instead of keyboards. For the burnout and attention angle, see [reducing physician burnout](/blog/reducing-physician-burnout). For evidence-oriented time savings, see [less time documenting with AI](/blog/less-time-documenting-ai-more-care).

## What ~20% note-time reduction actually means in a surgical week

Percentage improvements sound abstract until they hit a schedule. A 20% reduction in note-writing time does not magically create a free theatre list. It can mean:

- Shorter after-clinic catch-up
- Fewer weekends spent finishing letters
- Faster same-day note closure for handoffs
- More complete capture of counseling and consent discussions while details are fresh

Attendees in Lausanne also pushed on quality, not only speed. A faster wrong note is worse than a slower accurate one. That is why responsible AI documentation products emphasize editable drafts, specialty structure, and physician signature as the last step. Quality gains reported in early studies usually reflect more consistent sections and fewer omitted routine elements, not unsupervised autonomy.

### Risks that must stay on the slide deck

Dr. Gilson deliberately kept limitations visible:

- **Hallucinations and omissions**: plausible sentences that invent a detail or drop a critical negative
- **Clinical responsibility**: the signing physician remains accountable for the record
- **Data protection**: Swiss nFADP and EU GDPR expectations around processing, storage, and retention

Ignoring those risks is how pilots lose clinical sponsorship. Naming them early is how governance committees engage constructively. Our compliance overview on [GDPR and nFADP](/blog/docnote-gdpr-nfadp-compliance) maps the questions hospital counsel typically ask.

## Questions surgeons and hospital leaders asked in Lausanne

Side conversations after the talk clustered into themes that any Swiss department should answer before procurement.

### Can recordings stay local, and do they disappear?

Teams wanted concrete retention stories: where audio lives, whether it trains external models, and how automatic deletion works after a report is produced. Anonymisation and local storage were not buzzwords in Lausanne. They were entry tickets to serious evaluation. DocNote's Swiss-developed positioning, with emphasis on anonymised outputs and automatic deletion of recordings, spoke directly to that concern.

### How do we avoid "automation bias"?

If a draft looks polished, clinicians may under-edit. Faculty and quality leads asked for training that treats AI notes like trainee drafts: verify medications, laterality, allergies, diagnoses, and follow-up plans every time. Interface design should make edits cheap and acceptance deliberate.

### Will this help only ambulatory clinics?

Surgery lives across clinic, theatre, ward, and discharge. Leaders asked whether documentation AI covers operative reports and inpatient notes or only outpatient letters. A hospital-wide view matters for ROI and for residents who rotate across settings. That is the rationale behind [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/) spanning consultations, ward notes, operative reports, and discharge letters.

### What about multilingual Swiss practice?

Lausanne audiences work across French and German contexts (and often English research discourse). Speech recognition and note conventions must survive real accents and bilingual encounters, not only clean demo audio.

### How do we measure success without gaming the metric?

Time per note is necessary but not sufficient. Teams discussed pairing it with edit distance (how much the physician changes), safety incident review, patient-facing presence during visits, and staff-reported cognitive load.

## Adoption lessons for Swiss surgical services

Lausanne reinforced a playbook that travels well from university hospitals to regional services.

**Anchor the pilot in a pain everyone shares.** Outpatient clinic letters and postoperative notes are often better first targets than trying to automate the entire perioperative record on week one.

**Write the responsibility policy before the go-live date.** "Physician reviews and signs" should be a documented rule, taught in onboarding, and visible in the workflow.

**Bring privacy, DPO, and EHR teams into the same room.** Sequential approvals waste months. Parallel review of data flows and integration paths keeps clinical momentum.

**Prefer tools that delete raw audio by default.** Retention minimization reduces breach impact and simplifies patient communication.

**Publish internal lessons.** Surgical cultures learn from peers. A short internal report after 6 to 8 weeks (what failed, what improved, what templates changed) builds trust for phase two.

A sample Lausanne-inspired pilot outline:

1. 10 to 12 clinicians in one surgical clinic
2. Two document types with Swiss-adapted templates
3. Baseline measurement of note time and after-hours charting for 2 weeks
4. 6-week AI-assisted period with weekly case review of problematic drafts
5. Governance checkpoint: expand, redesign templates, or pause

## Specialty trends: from conference talks to Swiss hospital shortlists

Across 2025 and 2026, Swiss and European surgical meetings have shifted tone. AI documentation is no longer a side curiosity. It appears next to workforce planning and digital EHR modernization. Three trends stood out in Lausanne discussions and peer conversations afterward:

- **Ambient drafting becomes a clinician experience topic**, not only an IT experiment
- **Privacy localization becomes a purchasing filter**, especially for university hospitals
- **Hospital-wide document coverage beats single-note gadgets** when CMIO and CFO evaluate scale

That last point explains why presenting DocNote as more than a gadget mattered. Generating anonymised consultation reports with strict local storage and automatic deletion addresses the trust barrier. Expanding into broader [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/) addresses the scale barrier. Together they match what SCS audiences said they need: less admin time without compromising confidentiality.
