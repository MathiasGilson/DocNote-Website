DocNote took part in MedInTechs 2026, a major healthcare innovation event at the Parc Floral de Paris, as a partner of the Surgical Innovation Village. Over two days, the team presented an [AI medical scribe](/ai-medical-scribe/) built for hospital reality and exchanged with clinicians, entrepreneurs, and public stakeholders working to transform care delivery. The booth conversations were less about futuristic operating rooms alone and more about a practical constraint French hospitals share with peers across Europe: physicians drown in administrative tasks from pre-hospital and emergency settings through ward notes, discharge letters, and consultation reports.

![DocNote booth at MedInTechs 2026 in Paris](/images/medintechs_03.26_-_2.jpg)

A highlight of the event was the opportunity to introduce DocNote to former French ministers Valérie Pécresse and Olivier Véran, with special thanks to Muriel Benitah for the invitation and introductions. Public attention helps open doors. What sustains adoption is still the unglamorous detail clinicians asked about on the Village floor: EHR fit, liability, French and European data rules, and whether AI documentation works beyond a single outpatient demo. This article captures those themes so hospital teams who could not attend can still use the signal.

## Why the Surgical Innovation Village is a useful lens on AI adoption

Surgical innovation events traditionally spotlight devices, imaging, and robotics. Pairing that stage with documentation AI is not a category error. Surgery's value chain includes clinic triage, consent, operative reporting, inpatient follow-up, and discharge communication. If only the intraoperative minute is "innovated," the paperwork surrounding it still burns out teams and delays the next patient.

Village attendees moved fluidly between conversations about OR technology and conversations about time. That mix produced a clearer buying question than generic digital health fairs sometimes do:

- Does this tool change what happens to the patient in the next clinic slot or ward round?
- Can it survive CHU governance, not only a startup pitch jury?
- Is it designed for the full hospital pathway or for one narrow note type?

DocNote's positioning as an AI assistant for the administrative tasks physicians face across hospital settings matched that broader agenda. Interest was strongest when demos showed structured outputs for emergencies, ward follow-up, discharge letters, and consultations, not only a polished ambulatory SOAP note. For pathway scope, see [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/).

## What clinicians asked at the booth (and why it matters)

### 1. "Does it work in emergencies and on the ward, or only in clinic?"

French hospital physicians are wary of tools that optimize the easiest environment and ignore the rest of the shift. Emergency and inpatient documentation is interrupt-driven, multi-speaker, and full of handoffs. Teams wanted proof that ambient or assisted drafting can handle those conditions, then land cleanly in the record. That question separates toy scribes from hospital platforms.

### 2. "Where is the data, and who can train on it?"

GDPR literacy on the MedInTechs floor is high. Clinicians and innovation leads asked about hosting region, retention of audio, anonymisation, and subcontractors. HealthTech buyers increasingly treat "we are GDPR compliant" as incomplete unless deletion and training policies are explicit. Related reading: [DocNote GDPR and nFADP compliance](/blog/docnote-gdpr-nfadp-compliance).

### 3. "Who is liable if the discharge letter misses a line?"

Automation bias worries quality directors. The expected answer is unchanged: the physician validates and signs. Products earn trust when review is fast, differences are easy to spot, and the UI does not nudge blind acceptance.

### 4. "How do you integrate with our DPI / EHR world?"

A beautiful PDF that must be retyped is a non-starter for CHU scale. Integration pathways, export formats, and IT ownership models dominated second-day conversations with hospital digital teams.

### 5. "What is the change-management plan for skeptical seniors?"

Many seniors are open if the tool saves evening charting. They resist if it adds another login or threatens their note style. Successful pilots pair early adopters with respected clinical champions and allow template personalization.

## Policy interest and the public conversation in France

Meeting policymakers at an innovation salon is not the same as a procurement win. It does signal that documentation burden and AI in care are on the national innovation radar. French public debate often pairs AI enthusiasm with sovereignty and safety concerns. Hospital AI that keeps clinicians in control and data under European rules fits that narrative better than black-box tools with unclear offshore processing.

For teams translating political interest into institutional action, the durable work remains local:

1. Map document types that consume the most physician time
2. Align DPO, CMIO, and specialty heads on pilot criteria
3. Choose metrics that include safety review, not only speed
4. Budget for training and template iteration, not only licenses

Time-motion realities in French hospitals sharpen the case. Our article on [emergency physicians spending only a fraction of time with patients](/blog/hipaa-compliance-ai-tools) (drawing on CHU Bordeaux observational research) shows how documentation and coordination dominate shifts. AI will not fix bed capacity alone. It can return minutes that currently vanish into computers and phones.

## Adoption lessons from two days in the Village

**Lead with the hospital pathway, not the algorithm.** Clinicians leaned in when the story started from their day (consult, ward, discharge) and only then explained the model.

**Show failure modes in the demo.** Paradoxically, walking through a bad draft and a quick correction built more trust than a flawless scripted note.

**Separate surgical device buyers from documentation buyers.** Some Village visitors held capital budgets for OR equipment. Others owned clinical informatics. Know which conversation you are in.

**Capture objections in writing.** The best post-event artifact is not a badge scan list. It is a ranked list of concerns (integration, liability, languages, specialty templates) that product and clinical teams can answer in follow-up.

**Connect events into a narrative.** MedInTechs sits alongside other European stops. Readers comparing Paris conversations with Swiss surgical congress themes can also review [SCS Lausanne on admin burden and AI](/blog/scs-lausanne-may-2025) and [Adopt AI Paris](/blog/adopt-ai-paris-november-2025) for adjacent French ecosystem context.

A practical 90-day plan many French hospital innovators sketched after the show:

- **Days 1 to 30**: select service line, confirm data processing agreement topics, baseline metrics
- **Days 31 to 60**: limited user pilot with weekly morbidity-style review of documentation defects
- **Days 61 to 90**: decide expansion to a second specialty or pause for template redesign

## Specialty trends: surgical innovation is absorbing "soft" AI

Three trends were visible across Surgical Innovation Village sessions and aisles:

- **Robotics and imaging remain the marquee**, but workforce and documentation pain appear in almost every serious hospital conversation
- **AI scribes are evaluated as clinical infrastructure**, closer to EHR modules than to consumer gadgets
- **Cross-border European vendors are judged on localization**, including French clinical language, hospital document genres, and EU data residency

DocNote's message at MedInTechs matched those trends: automate the administrative span of hospital physician work, from pre-hospital and emergencies to ward follow-up notes, discharge letters, and consultation reports, while keeping clinicians accountable for the signed record. Strong interest throughout the show reflected hunger for that full-span approach.
