At the annual SSMIG Congress (Swiss Society of General Internal Medicine / SGAIM), one of Switzerland's leading gatherings for internal medicine and primary care, DocNote was presented as a practical answer to a familiar problem: how to cut administrative burden without weakening clinical quality or data protection. Interest in [AI medical documentation](/ai-medical-scribe/) is no longer theoretical in Swiss outpatient and hospital practice. Clinicians want tools that fit real consultation rhythms, respect Swiss and EU privacy rules, and leave the physician in control of every note that enters the record.

![DocNote at SSMIG annual conference](/images/ssmig_-_2.jpg)

As part of the congress program, the project was highlighted during the Prix Lumière, an award recognizing forward-thinking initiatives that aim to improve quality of care and physicians' working conditions. That framing matters. It placed AI documentation next to other structural responses to burnout, access, and continuity of care, not next to speculative technology demos. The conversations around the presentation revealed where Swiss internal medicine is open to adoption, where skepticism is healthy, and which design choices separate a useful scribe from another screen that steals attention.

## Why SSMIG is a natural stage for documentation innovation

General internal medicine sits at the center of Swiss care pathways. Physicians manage multimorbidity, coordinate specialists, document longitudinal histories, and absorb rising coding and traceability demands. When note-writing expands, the cost shows up as shorter visits, delayed letters, evening "pajama time," and less eye contact with patients.

Congress attendees repeatedly linked documentation load to three pressures:

- **Volume**: more chronic conditions, more follow-up, more multidisciplinary correspondence
- **Fragmentation**: EHR screens that force copy-paste, duplicate data entry, and after-visit catch-up
- **Accountability**: medico-legal expectations that every decision be reconstructible from the chart

Those pressures explain why ambient and assisted documentation keep appearing on Swiss medical agendas. They also explain why adoption is cautious. Internal medicine teams do not need another generic chatbot. They need structured outputs that match consultation styles, specialty templates, and hospital or practice EHRs. ## What the Prix Lumière framing signaled about clinical priorities

The Prix Lumière context was not a product pitch in isolation. It framed AI documentation as a working-conditions and quality-of-care topic. That is how many Swiss clinicians evaluate new tools: Does this reduce cognitive load? Does it preserve the physician-patient relationship? Does it create new liability or privacy risk?

During and after the presentation, the discussion returned to measurable benefits that matter in primary care and hospital internal medicine:

- Reduced time spent drafting consultation notes and follow-up letters
- More complete capture of history, medications, and plan details while the conversation is still fresh
- Better ability to face the patient instead of the keyboard during the encounter
- Clearer handoffs when notes are structured and consistent across clinicians

Those benefits only count if the tool stays clinically relevant. Attendees were equally interested in limitations: hallucinations, overconfident phrasing, incomplete medication lists, and the risk of "accepting" a draft without a proper review. Responsible deployment starts from that honesty. AI can draft. The physician validates, edits, and signs.

## Questions Swiss internists asked about AI scribes

Event conversations are often more revealing than slide decks. At SSMIG, questions clustered around five themes that any hospital or practice evaluating an [AI medical scribe](/ai-medical-scribe/) should answer before a pilot.

### 1. Where does the audio go, and for how long?

Swiss and EU data protection rules make storage location, retention, and deletion policies first-order concerns. Clinicians asked whether recordings leave Switzerland or the EU, whether transcripts are reused for model training, and how quickly raw audio is deleted after a note is generated. Clear answers, local storage options, and automatic deletion policies are no longer "nice to have." They are prerequisites for trust. For the compliance angle in European deployments, read our note on [DocNote GDPR and nFADP compliance](/blog/docnote-gdpr-nfadp-compliance).

### 2. Who is responsible if the note is wrong?

Medico-legal responsibility does not transfer to a vendor because a draft was generated automatically. Physicians wanted explicit workflows that keep human review mandatory, show what the AI proposed, and make edits easy before the note lands in the EHR. The healthiest mental model is the same as for a junior colleague drafting a letter: helpful, never unsupervised.

### 3. Will this work for complex multimorbidity visits?

Primary care and internal medicine visits often jump between problems, social context, and medication reconciliation. Attendees wanted reassurance that AI documentation can handle multi-problem encounters without flattening nuance into a single vague assessment. Specialty-aware structure and editable sections matter more here than flashy demos on short, single-complaint visits.

### 4. How does it connect to the systems we already use?

A scribe that creates beautiful notes outside the EHR creates a second inbox. Integration, export paths, and hospital-wide document types (ward notes, discharge letters, consultation reports) came up repeatedly. Documentation innovation only scales when it meets clinicians where they already work. That is the core promise behind [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/) workflows that cover more than the outpatient room.

### 5. What about languages and Swiss clinical reality?

Switzerland's multilingual practice environment is not an edge case. French, German, Italian, and mixed encounters are routine. Teams asked whether speech recognition and note generation hold up across languages and accents, and whether templates can match local report conventions rather than US-centric SOAP defaults alone.

## Adoption lessons for primary care and hospital internal medicine

Interest at SSMIG does not equal overnight rollout. The practices and departments most likely to succeed with AI documentation share a few patterns that emerged in side conversations and mirrored what we see across Swiss and European deployments.

**Start with a defined document type.** Consultation reports or discharge letters with clear structure beat "document everything" pilots. Scope creates measurable before-and-after comparisons.

**Keep physicians in the edit loop from day one.** Training should emphasize review habits, red-flag checking (allergies, dosages, diagnoses), and personal style preferences. Speed without review is not a win.

**Measure what clinicians feel, not only clicks.** Time per note matters. So do after-hours charting, same-day note closure, and subjective cognitive load. Burnout reduction is partly about reclaiming attention. Our related article on [reducing physician burnout](/blog/reducing-physician-burnout) expands on that link.

**Treat privacy as a clinical feature.** When staff can explain retention and deletion in one sentence, trust rises and governance reviews move faster.

**Plan for mixed seniority.** Residents may draft more with AI. Senior physicians may want tighter templates. Both groups need the same accountability standard.

A practical pilot checklist many teams left the congress discussing looked like this:

1. Choose one specialty unit or practice pod (8 to 15 clinicians)
2. Define 2 to 3 document types and success metrics for 6 to 8 weeks
3. Confirm data residency, retention, and EHR handoff before go-live
4. Run weekly feedback rounds on hallucinations, missing fields, and template gaps
5. Decide expand / iterate / pause with clinical leadership, not only IT

## Specialty trends: why internal medicine is moving from curiosity to pilots

Across Europe, ambient documentation started in ambulatory clinics and is now entering hospital wards, emergency pathways, and specialty clinics. Internal medicine is a natural early adopter for three reasons:

- Visits are conversation-heavy and documentation-heavy at the same time
- Continuity of care depends on notes other clinicians can trust
- Administrative load is a known driver of early career dissatisfaction

The trend is not "replace the chart." It is "draft the chart from the encounter, then refine." That shift aligns with broader interest in responsible clinical AI: tools that remove repetitive work while leaving judgment, empathy, and final accountability with the physician. DocNote's presence at SSMIG reflected that shift inside Swiss internal medicine: growing demand for AI that is clinically relevant, secure, and integrated into everyday workflows rather than parked in a side portal.

Hospitals evaluating wider rollout often ask how outpatient scribing extends to inpatient notes and discharge communication. That is exactly the gap a [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/) approach is meant to close, from consultation reports through ward follow-up and discharge letters.
