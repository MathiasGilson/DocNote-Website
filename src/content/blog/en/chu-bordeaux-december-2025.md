At the CHU HealthTech Connexion Day, a key event dedicated to hospital innovation, startup DocNote presented its AI assistant designed to automate physicians' administrative workflows. University hospital audiences are among the toughest and most valuable testers of digital health claims. They live inside complex EHRs, multi-specialty pathways, and strict data protection review. Interest in an [AI medical scribe](/ai-medical-scribe/) only converts when the tool looks like hospital infrastructure, not a side app for a single outpatient niche.

![DocNote at CHU HealthTech Connexion Day](/images/bordeaux_12.25_-_2.jpg)

The solution generates structured clinical documents, including outpatient consultations, inpatient ward notes, operative reports, and discharge letters, from recorded conversations and uploaded medical documents. Unlike tools focused solely on outpatient care, DocNote positions itself as a comprehensive hospital-wide approach, with direct integration into electronic health record (EHR) systems. In Bordeaux, discussions centered on time savings, workflow efficiency, and data protection compliance under mounting pressure on healthcare teams. That combination is the real story of the day: CHUs want AI embedded in everyday practice, governed like any other clinical system.

## Why university hospitals evaluate documentation AI differently

A CHU is not a single clinic with one template. It is a federation of services sharing infrastructure, risk, and reputation. Innovation leads and department heads therefore ask questions ambulatory-only vendors under-prepare for:

- Will this work for ward rounds and discharge, not only scheduled consults?
- Who owns the integration with our EHR and identity stack?
- How do we train rotating residents without multiplying accounts and styles?
- What does the DPO need in writing before a pilot starts?
- How do we measure benefit in minutes and in safety?

DocNote's Bordeaux presence spoke to that checklist by emphasizing hospital document breadth and EHR-oriented design. For the product scope behind those conversations, see [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/).

## The time pressure CHUs already measure

French university hospitals do not need abstract burnout essays to know documentation competes with bedside care. Observational work at Bordeaux itself has shown how little of an emergency shift may be face-to-face care when communication, computer documentation, and phone coordination dominate the clock. We unpack that research in [our piece on how little emergency physician time reaches the bedside](/blog/hipaa-compliance-ai-tools). The Connexion Day conversations echoed the same structural problem across specialties: expertise is scarce, administrative load is not.

AI documentation will not open new beds by itself. It can reduce re-typing, accelerate letter closure, and cut after-hours charting when drafts are good enough to edit quickly. Hospitals that treat those minutes as a workforce intervention, not a gadget trial, set better success criteria.

## What Bordeaux attendees asked at the stand

### Hospital-wide versus outpatient-only

The most repeated differentiator question was blunt: "Is this another ambulatory scribe?" Teams leaned in when they heard coverage for ward notes, operative reports, and discharge letters alongside consultations. CHU ROI often appears when residents and attendings use one pattern across settings.

### EHR integration as a first-class feature

Innovation officers asked about integration approaches early, sometimes before they asked about model quality. That order is rational. A high-quality note stranded outside the DPI still creates double work. Direct EHR integration pathways were therefore central to the DocNote narrative in Bordeaux.

### Data protection without hand-waving

Compliance questions covered hosting, subprocessors, retention of recordings, access controls, and whether clinical audio could train external models. University hospitals expect vendor answers that survive legal and CNIL-aware review. European-focused guidance is outlined in [DocNote GDPR and nFADP compliance](/blog/docnote-gdpr-nfadp-compliance).

### Uploaded documents plus conversation

Physicians rarely document from speech alone. They reconcile referral letters, labs, and prior reports. Attendees responded to workflows that combine recorded conversations with uploaded medical documents to produce structured outputs, because that matches real chart assembly.

### Change management across specialties

A radiology template is not a visceral surgery discharge letter. CHU leaders wanted specialty configuration and governance for prompt or template changes so quality does not fragment service by service.

## Adoption lessons for CHU and large hospital groups

**Create a cross-functional pilot board on day zero.** Clinical champion, CMIO or digital health lead, DPO, EHR IT, and a quality representative should share one scorecard.

**Pick a service line with measurable letter delay.** Outpatient specialty clinics and discharge-heavy wards are common starting points. Avoid boiling the ocean across 30 departments in month one.

**Define "done" for a document.** Example: signed consultation note in EHR same day, with physician edit log retained for audit sampling.

**Budget training like a clinical protocol.** Two short sessions beat a single launch email. Include how to handle bad drafts and when to discard and rewrite.

**Plan the second wave before you celebrate the first.** If clinic notes work, ward and discharge documents need their own templates and success metrics.

A CHU-oriented 12-week outline many visitors discussed:

1. **Weeks 1 to 2**: DPIA inputs, DPA topics, EHR sandbox access, baseline metrics
2. **Weeks 3 to 8**: limited production pilot in one or two units
3. **Weeks 9 to 10**: quality sampling and template redesign
4. **Weeks 11 to 12**: scale / iterate / stop decision with hospital governance

## Specialty trends: HealthTech days are shifting from demos to deployment criteria

HealthTech Connexion formats across French CHUs increasingly reward vendors who can discuss deployment criteria with the same fluency as demos. Three trends stood out in Bordeaux:

- **Hospital-wide documentation platforms outrank single-note toys** in serious evaluation conversations
- **EHR integration and identity are purchasing filters**, not phase-two wishes
- **Data protection is part of clinical safety**, because trust failures kill adoption even when models are competent

A fourth undercurrent is worth naming: **resident workflows as the scale path**. Attendings decide whether a tool is trustworthy. Residents often decide whether it becomes habitual across night shifts and rotations. CHU pilots that ignore onboarding for juniors see uneven uptake and inconsistent note quality. Pilots that teach both groups the same review discipline tend to spread faster between services.

DocNote's exhibit highlighted growing interest among university hospitals in AI solutions embedded directly into everyday clinical practice. That interest is conditional. Embed means structured documents that clinicians will actually sign, systems they already open, and policies their institutions can defend. It also means supporting the unglamorous middle of hospital life: the ward note written between interruptions, the discharge letter that unblocks a bed, the operative report that the next team actually reads.

### What "embedded" looks like in practice

When Bordeaux visitors translated interest into next-step language, they usually described a short chain:

1. Capture the encounter or upload source documents
2. Generate a structured draft matched to the document type
3. Edit under physician responsibility
4. Land the signed note in the EHR without retyping

Any break in that chain becomes a reason to abandon the tool after the novelty week. That is why Connexion Day conversations spent as much time on integration and governance as on speech recognition accuracy.
