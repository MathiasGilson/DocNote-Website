At the invitation of Professor Léo Bühler and the Association for Research in Surgery, Dr. Alice Gilson, co-founder of DocNote, spoke at the Annual Congress of the Swiss College of Surgeons in Lucerne. She contributed to the session "AI, CAR-T Cells and Omics in the OR: Will Surgeons Still Be There?", a title that captures anxiety and curiosity in equal measure. The honest answer from the stage was clearer than the provocation suggests: surgeons are not disappearing. The profession is evolving, and [AI medical documentation](/ai-medical-scribe/) is one of the places where that evolution already saves time outside the operating room.

The talk explored how AI is already transforming surgery and healthcare inside the OR through robotics, image-guided surgery, and decision-support systems, and outside it through research, data analysis, and clinical documentation. Far from replacing clinical judgment, technical expertise, or the human connection with patients, AI can remove repetitive administrative tasks and free capacity for operative care, teaching, and follow-up. What Lucerne made visible is how Swiss surgical communities are refining that message into practical questions about adoption, specialty fit, and trust.

## The real question behind "Will surgeons still be there?"

Headline sessions about AI and the future of surgery often polarize into utopia or replacement fear. Practicing surgeons in Lucerne largely rejected both extremes. Their questions were operational:

- Which AI tools already change preoperative planning or intraoperative guidance?
- Which tools only add alert fatigue?
- How do we keep accountability when algorithms suggest a plan or draft a note?
- Where should a department invest first when capital, training time, and IT bandwidth are limited?

That last question is where documentation enters a surgery congress that is otherwise dominated by OR technology. Operative excellence still depends on what happens before and after the case: clinic visits, consent discussions, ward rounds, discharge letters, and multidisciplinary correspondence. When those tasks expand, surgeons either stay late or compress patient conversation. AI that drafts structured notes does not make a surgeon optional. It makes the surgeon more available for the work only a surgeon can do.

## Inside the OR: where AI is tangible today

Dr. Gilson's session placed documentation in a broader map of surgical AI. Attendees recognized progress in several domains that already touch daily practice:

- **Preoperative planning**: automated segmentation and 3D reconstruction that help visualize anatomy before incision
- **Intraoperative navigation and imaging**: real-time overlays and image analysis that support precision
- **Predictive monitoring**: models that flag risk trajectories during and after procedures
- **Robotics and assisted platforms**: systems that extend dexterity and standardization, still under surgeon control

These tools change technique and training. They do not erase the need for judgment when anatomy is atypical, when comorbidities stack, or when a plan must change mid-case. The Lucerne conversation treated that continuity as a feature of responsible AI, not a marketing footnote.

### Outside the OR: research, data, and the paperwork tax

Beyond the sterile field, AI supports literature synthesis, outcome analysis, and trial logistics. Yet the pain point surgeons raised most consistently in hallway discussions was still administrative load. Clinic templates, operative reports, and discharge communication compete with teaching and theatre time. Early evidence that ambient tools can cut note time and after-hours charting resonates because surgical schedules leave little slack. ## What surgeons asked after the session

Q&A and booth conversations after a future-of-surgery talk tend to be sharper than polite applause. In Lucerne, recurring themes included:

### Accountability and the surgical signature

Who owns an AI-drafted operative note if a detail is wrong? Surgeons wanted workflows that make review unavoidable and edits straightforward. The professional norm should stay familiar: the attending (or responsible surgeon) validates the record, just as they validate a trainee's draft.

### Specialty nuance in documentation

Visceral, orthopedic, cardiac, and outpatient procedural practices do not share one note shape. Teams asked whether AI documentation can respect specialty templates, bilingual Swiss contexts, and hospital report conventions rather than forcing a generic ambulatory format. Flexible structure is an adoption requirement, not a cosmetic preference.

### Data protection in high-stakes environments

Operating lists and clinic recordings contain sensitive identifiers and clinical detail. Questions about local storage, retention, deletion of audio, and whether data trains external models came quickly. Swiss surgical departments expect answers that survive hospital legal review. Our overview of [DocNote GDPR and nFADP compliance](/blog/docnote-gdpr-nfadp-compliance) covers the European frame many teams referenced.

### Integration with hospital systems

A draft that lives in a separate app creates friction. Surgeons asked about EHR handoff for consultation notes, operative reports, ward updates, and discharge letters. That is the difference between a demo and a [hospital documentation](/hospital-documentation/) workflow.

### Training the next generation

If residents rely on AI drafts, will they still learn to write a clear operative narrative? Faculty interest centered on using AI as a scaffold that still requires critique, not as a bypass of clinical reasoning. Good pilots include teaching moments: what the model missed, what must never be auto-accepted, how to phrase uncertainty.

## Adoption lessons for surgical departments

Departments that move from conference inspiration to a controlled pilot usually follow a sequence that Lucerne attendees discussed in practical terms.

**Separate OR AI from documentation AI in the business case.** Robotics and imaging capital projects have different timelines, vendors, and safety cases than ambient scribing. Mixing them in one "AI budget" slows both.

**Pilot documentation where conversation volume is high.** Outpatient surgical clinics and postoperative follow-up often yield faster wins than trying to automate every intraoperative nuance on day one.

**Define document types and metrics up front.** Examples: time to finalize clinic notes, same-day closure rate, edits required per draft, and surgeon-reported cognitive load.

**Keep humans in the loop as policy, not slogan.** Mandatory review, visible draft provenance, and easy correction pathways should be part of the protocol clinicians sign.

**Involve coding, quality, and privacy early.** Surgical documentation touches billing codes, morbidity review, and legal discovery. Surprises after go-live are expensive.

A lightweight 60-day pilot shape many teams sketched after the congress:

1. Select one clinic or service line (for example visceral surgery outpatient)
2. Onboard a mixed group of attendings and residents
3. Enable 2 document types with specialty templates
4. Hold fortnightly review of failure cases (missing allergies, wrong laterality language, incomplete plans)
5. Decide whether to expand to ward notes and discharge letters

## Specialty trends: surgery's AI stack is becoming layered

The Lucerne session sat AI documentation next to CAR-T, omics, and OR innovation for a reason. Surgical futures are layered:

- **Molecular and cellular therapies** change who is a surgical candidate and how perioperative pathways are planned
- **Imaging and robotics** change how operations are performed
- **Documentation and research AI** change how knowledge and records keep up with that complexity

None of these layers remove the surgeon. They change the skill mix. Communication, consent, intraoperative judgment, and postoperative ownership remain human. Administrative drafting is the layer most ready for safe automation today because the output is editable text under professional signature, not an irreversible robotic move.

Swiss College of Surgeons audiences are also watching peer societies abroad. The pattern is consistent: start where evidence and workflow fit are strongest, publish local experience, and resist tools that cannot explain data handling. ## Conclusion: keep the surgeon central, remove the paperwork drag

The SCS Lucerne session asked a dramatic question and answered it with a grounded one. Surgeons will still be there. The open issue is whether their time is consumed by recoverable administrative work or protected for operative care and patients. AI in the OR and AI in documentation are different investments, but they share a principle: augment expertise, do not outsource accountability.
